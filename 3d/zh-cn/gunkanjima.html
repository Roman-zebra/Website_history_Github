    关于军舰岛的常见问题

    军舰岛是什么？

    军舰岛是长崎港外约15公里处煤矿岛“端岛”的俗称。南北约480米、东西约160米，面积约6.3公顷，从明治末年到1931年经六次填海在护岸内扩建而成。1960年人口普查有5,267人居住。

    军舰岛从何时起无人居住？

    三菱的煤矿于1974年1月15日关闭，同年4月20日最后一批居民离岛。此后一直无人居住。

    30号楼是什么？

    1916年建成的30号楼被认为是日本第一座钢筋混凝土高层公寓。建成时为4层，后加建到7层，中间有天井。

    可以登上军舰岛吗？

    可以。从长崎港出发的登岛团自2009年4月22日起运营。在岛上只能走东南侧的参观通道，浪高、风速或能见度超过长崎市标准的日子不能登岛。

    军舰岛是世界遗产吗？

    端岛作为“明治日本的产业革命遗产”的组成部分，于2015年7月5日列入世界遗产。列入时日本政府代表表示，1940年代在部分设施有许多朝鲜半岛出身者等人违背本人意愿被带来、在严酷环境下被迫劳动。

    这个3D是怎么做的？

    用1962年和2010年各两张航拍照片做影像匹配测出高度，建筑按轮廓、层数和竣工年画成棱柱，放在日本国土地理院5米高程的地面上。墙面是按类型画的示意图，不是照片。局限见本页的“注意事项”。

    Five thousand people on a rock: Gunkanjima from the air, 1962 to today

    A rock in the sea

    Ava Four hundred and eighty metres long, a hundred and sixty wide. You could walk round it in fifteen minutes. And at one point, more than five thousand people lived on it.

    Andrew Five thousand two hundred and sixty-seven people in 1960. That's about eighty-three thousand people per square kilometre. Nine times the density of central Tokyo at the time. People called it the most crowded place on earth, and they were probably right.

    Ava This is Hashima, off Nagasaki. Everyone calls it Gunkanjima. Battleship Island.

    Andrew And today we're going to look at it the way a survey plane saw it on the thirtieth of May, 1962. Then we'll raise it into 3D and walk through what's there.

    Two photographs, one shape

    Ava Okay, first question. How do you get 3D out of a photo from 1962?

    Andrew The plane took two photographs of the island, about a kilometre apart. Same island, two viewpoints.

    Ava Right.

    Andrew Tall things shift more between the two frames than low things. Hold up a finger, close one eye, then the other. The finger jumps. Measure that jump for every point on the island, and you've got its height.

    Ava And you trust it?

    Andrew We checked it two ways. The flying height worked out from the map scale is one thousand nine hundred and fifty metres. The plane's own altimeter is printed on the edge of the photograph, and it says exactly one nine five zero. And a floating pier moored by the island comes out about two and a half metres above the water, which is roughly what a floating pier should be.

    Ava Then let's raise it.

    Andrew One honest note. This version has proper walls. The outlines come from OpenStreetMap, the heights from the photographs. But the rows of windows are drawn from the number of storeys. They're a sketch, not something the camera saw.

    Ava Noted.

    An island built outwards

    Ava The first thing I see is the wall, all the way round.

    Andrew That sea wall is why the island has this shape at all. Hashima started as a much smaller reef. Between the late Meiji years and 1931 it was enlarged six times, and the new land sits behind that wall.

    Ava Is that where the name comes from? The battleship?

    Andrew Apparently a newspaper in the 1920s wrote that it looked like the battleship Tosa, and it stuck. From the sea, the wall and the tall blocks really do read as a warship.

    Ava And the reason to build out here was coal.

    Andrew Coal under the seabed. The story goes it was found around 1810, and Mitsubishi bought the island in 1890 for a hundred thousand yen. Low ash, low sulphur. It sold well.

    Building upwards

    Ava So where do you put the miners?

    Andrew Up. Find the square block with a hole in the middle, at the foot of the rock in the south-west. That's Building Number Thirty, finished in 1916. It's usually described as Japan's first high-rise apartment block in reinforced concrete.

    Ava 1916. Tokyo didn't have anything like that yet.

    Andrew It opened with four storeys and they added three more later. And it didn't stop there. Along the west side, facing the open sea, runs a line of miners' housing, Buildings Sixteen to Twenty, from 1918 onwards.

    Ava So the housing is also the breakwater.

    Andrew And the ground floor was Hashima Ginza: the greengrocer, the fishmonger, the sake shop, a pawnbroker, and a market every day of traders who came over by boat.

    Ava I heard there were vegetable plots on the roofs.

    Andrew On Buildings Eighteen and Nineteen. They carried soil up, the children grew tomatoes and aubergines, and rice in the autumn. There was a harvest festival in 1966.

    Andrew In a typhoon, people said the waves came over the roof.

    Life in 1962

    Ava In this 1962 photograph, what's the island like?

    Andrew At its fullest. Look at the north end, the big U-shaped block. That's Building Sixty-Five. Nine storeys, three hundred and seventeen flats. They started the north wing in 1945 and kept adding until 1958. The courtyard was the playground, and the kindergarten was on the roof.

    Ava A kindergarten on the roof.

    Andrew A nursery, built on the roof of the east wing in 1953 for a hundred and fifty children. The garden pond the children helped build and the iron slide are still up there.

    Andrew No ground to spare. Right next to it is the school, seven storeys. Built in 1957 with six, one more added in 1961. Its biggest year ever was 1962: one thousand one hundred and sixty-nine pupils.

    Ava The same year as our photograph.

    Andrew The very same. This photo catches the island at its peak.

    Andrew There was a hospital too: Building Sixty-Nine, just north of the school. Four storeys, fifty-eight beds, an operating theatre and an X-ray room.

    Ava And baths?

    Andrew Communal baths. The lower bath in the basement of Building Sixty-One took twenty people at a time. Free, open from three to eight in the evening. Only the managers in Building Three and the club had baths of their own.

    Ava There's something on top of the rock in the middle.

    Andrew Hashima Shrine, built in 1936. Every third of April the whole island turned out for the festival, and the highlight was the portable shrine being run down a staircase so steep that people called it the stairway to hell. The wooden hall has collapsed. What's left up there now is a small stone shrine.

    Ava That's the little point on the skyline when you see it from the sea.

    The mine

    Ava And the mine itself?

    Andrew South-east side. The number two shaft was the main way down, and the men climbed a flight of stairs to reach the pithead. It's filled with concrete now. Right beside it stand brick walls from a winding-engine house built in 1896, one of the oldest things on the island.

    Ava Brick from 1896, concrete from 1916. The island comes in layers.

    A history to remember

    Andrew There's one more layer we should name. When these industrial sites joined the World Heritage List in 2015, Japan's representative told the committee that there were a large number of Koreans and others who were brought against their will and forced to work under harsh conditions in the 1940s at some of the sites. Hashima is one of those sites.

    Ava So we hold both. The engineering story, and the people who never chose to be here.

    Andrew You can't tell this island with only one of them.

    After the people left

    Ava What happened next?

    Andrew Oil replaced coal. The mine closed on the fifteenth of January 1974, and on the twentieth of April the last residents took the boat. The island was empty.

    Ava Let me move the slider. Here's January 1975, in colour.

    Andrew One year after closing, and from above it still looks lived in. The buildings are all there, so for this year we keep the 1962 shape.

    Ava And 2010.

    Andrew For 2010 we measured the heights again, from a new pair of photographs. Switch on the change colours. Red is where the island is at least five metres lower than in 1962. Blue is where it's higher.

    Ava The big concrete blocks are mostly still standing. But a lot has come down.

    Andrew Altogether about 6,900 square metres read lower. Mostly where the wooden buildings were, and along the sea wall.

    Ava And the latest photograph?

    Andrew Greener. And still standing, just.

    Visiting today

    Ava Can you actually go?

    Andrew Yes, on a landing tour from Nagasaki port. The public has been allowed to land since the twenty-second of April 2009. You stay on the visitor walkway, and if the waves, the wind or the visibility are outside the city's limits, nobody lands that day.

    Ava Will it last?

    Andrew Nagasaki City has a thirty-year conservation plan. The sea wall comes first, then the mine remains. For the housing, they're starting with Building Number Three, up on the highest ground.

    Ava What's Number Three?

    Andrew The managers' housing. Best view on the island, and in better shape than most. So that's where they begin.

    Your turn

    Ava That's the island. Everything you just heard is on this page. Turn it, slide through the years, tap the buildings.

    Andrew The photographs come from the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan. The model is a first attempt, so it's a few metres out in places. And our voices are Microsoft's synthetic voices. We're not real people. The script was written from the sources on this page.

    Ava See you on the next island.

    注意事项

    • 有些地方高度会差几米；这是帮助理解这座岛的复原，不是测量图。
    • 建筑是复原：轮廓和层数有出处，但墙面是按类型画的示意图，竣工年不明的建筑从1950年起显示。1947年只显示竣工年已知的建筑，所以那一年的景象并不完整。
    • 楼梯间、烟囱、输送机架和楼间的小屋没有做出来；地形是5米网格，所以岩山比实际更平滑。
    • 无法匹配的点，1962年大约每六个点有一个，2010年大约每十七个点有一个，都用周围的数值补上。
    • 1975年和最新的画面借用了其他年份的形状，因此在这期间倒塌的建筑在模型中仍然立着。
    • 为了便于比较，调整过照片的颜色。
