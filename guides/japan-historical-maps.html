Japan Historical Maps & Old Aerial Photos: How to Compare Then and Now

Open the map

Japan Time Atlas lays old aerial photographs from the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (GSI) under today’s map. This guide covers which photographs you are looking at, how to line them up, and why some spots show nothing.

The four photo layers

  • Aerial photographs, 1945–1950 (GSI tile ID ort_USA10), zoom levels 10–17. GSI’s holdings include photographs taken by the US military around 1945. They do not cover the whole country.
  • Aerial photographs, 1961–1969 (ort_old10), zoom levels 10–17.
  • National land image information, 1974–1978 (gazo1), zoom levels 10–17. These are colour photographs.
  • Latest seamless photographs (seamlessphoto), taken from 2007 onwards, zoom levels 14–18. A recent photograph, not a live view.

When you open a spot, the app looks for the oldest of these series that actually has a photograph there, and puts that year on the comparison button.

Line up the two photographs first

  1. Open a place from the list, or search for a spot on the map, and press the Compare button.
  2. Before you look for change, find one thing that appears in both photographs: a river bend, a moat, a railway line, the edge of a temple’s grounds. Put the dividing line right on top of it.
  3. Drag the line slowly back and forth across that feature. When the two halves meet cleanly you are looking at the same ground, and differences elsewhere are worth a closer look.
  4. If you lose your bearings, zoom out one step, find the river or the coast again, and zoom back in.

The year on the button is a series, not a date

“1945–1950” covers several years of photography. A frame in that series may have been taken in any of those years, and the map does not say which. When the exact date matters, look the photograph up in GSI’s Map and Aerial Photo Viewing Service.

The same goes for the latest layer. “Latest” means the most recent photograph GSI has published for that area, which can be several years old.

What to look for, place by place

  • Hiroshima (1945–1950). Start at the T-shaped Aioi Bridge. South of it, between the Motoyasu and Honkawa rivers, the old photograph shows mostly bare ground where the Nakajima district stood. Today it is Peace Memorial Park.
  • Yokohama (1945–1950). Minato Mirai 21 was built after the Mitsubishi Heavy Industries Yokohama Shipyard moved out: the move was completed in March 1983 and construction began that November. Compare the shoreline and the shipyard with today’s parks and towers.
  • Kobe (1945–1950). Meriken Park was made in 1987 by reclaiming the water between Meriken Pier and Naka Pier. About 60 metres of quay damaged in the 1995 earthquake are kept as they were, in the Port of Kobe Earthquake Memorial Park.
  • Tokyo, Asakusa (1945–1950). Senso-ji’s main hall burned in the air raid of 10 March 1945 and was rebuilt in 1958, so a photograph from 1945–1950 shows the grounds before the present hall was built.
  • Himeji (1961–1969). The main keep was taken apart and rebuilt between 1956 and March 1964. Just south of it, the open lawn of Sannomaru Square is covered with large buildings in the old photograph.
  • Nagasaki (1961–1969). The old layer is from the 1960s, so it shows the city after reconstruction had begun, not the weeks after 9 August 1945.
  • Hashima (Gunkanjima) (1961–1969). The island while people still lived there, packed with buildings, against today’s photograph with plants spreading over it.

Why a spot can come up blank

  • No photograph covers it. The older series have gaps. Where the 1945–1950 series has nothing, the app tries the next series, and if none of them covers the spot, the map tells you.
  • You have zoomed in past the photograph. The old series go up to zoom level 17 and the latest photograph to 18.
  • The ground is hidden. Cloud, haze or deep shadow in a valley can hide the ground in an individual photograph.
  • Not much changed. Forest, sea and farmland can look much the same in both halves. That is a result, not an error.

Reading change carefully

Season, time of day and resolution all change how a place looks from the air. A building can seem to disappear under a shadow or a tree without having gone anywhere. Use the photographs to ask questions, and check specific historical claims against written sources.
