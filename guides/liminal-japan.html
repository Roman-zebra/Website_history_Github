Liminal Spaces in Japan: Unusual Stations, Retro Places & Industrial Heritage

Open the map

A liminal space is somewhere built for passing through, met when nobody else is there. The word describes a feeling, not a type of building.

In Japan that can be a working station at a quiet hour, a shopping building from 1966 or an island nobody has lived on since 1974. Before you add one to a trip, work out which kind of place it is, because that decides whether you can go and how.

1. Still a working station

Doai Station, Gunma is an unstaffed JR East station on the Joetsu Line. Its northbound platform is about 70 metres underground: 462 steps down to the platform and 24 along the connecting passage, 486 in all, and about ten minutes on foot. Only about five trains stop each way a day, so plan around the timetable.

On the map: the station building sits beside the road. The platform you will use never appears, because it is inside the mountain.

2. Still a working building

Nakano Broadway, Tokyo opened in 1966 with shops on the lower floors and homes above. About 200 shops fill the basement to the fourth floor. It is a lived-in, working building, not a ruin.

On the map: Broadway stands at the end of the Nakano Sun Mall shopping arcade, a few minutes’ walk from the north exit of Nakano Station.

3. Now a visitor site

Ōya History Museum, Utsunomiya lets you walk into a former underground quarry for Ōya stone: about 20,000 square metres, 30 metres below ground on average and 60 metres at the deepest, and around 8°C inside. It has set opening hours and charges admission, and from December to March it closes on Tuesdays.

On the map: the underground space cannot be seen from the air; the photograph only shows the ground above it. Bring something warm to wear.

4. Tour only

Hashima (Gunkanjima), Nagasaki can be visited only on a landing tour run by a boat operator from Nagasaki. Landings do not go ahead when wave height, wind speed or visibility are outside the limits set by Nagasaki City, and on the island you stay within the visitor facilities.

On the map: compare the 1960s photograph, taken while people still lived on the island, with today’s, where plants have spread over much of it.

5. Do not enter

Closed hotels, ruins, fenced sites and private land. Look at them from public roads, and if a sign says keep out, keep out.

On the map: check how the site meets public roads and stay on them. A pin on this map is not permission to go inside.

Before you go anywhere

  • Check the operator’s or the municipality’s own page for opening days, closures and access on the day you plan to go.
  • A place that looks empty in a photograph may be full of people when you arrive.
  • Respect residents, staff and other visitors, and follow photography rules where they are posted.
