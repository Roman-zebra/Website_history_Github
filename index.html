Questions

What did Japan look like in 1945?

You can see it directly. This map shows the aerial photographs the US military flew over Japan between 1945 and 1950, beside today's imagery, with a slider between them. Hiroshima, Tokyo, Osaka, Sapporo, Nagoya, Sendai and Fukuoka all have 1945 coverage.

How can I see Hiroshima before and after the atomic bomb?

Open the Hiroshima entry and drag the slider. The 1945 photograph shows the flattened city around the one surviving dome; today the same ground is a park. The 1945-1950 survey was taken after the bombing, so it shows the aftermath rather than the city beforehand.

Are there old aerial photographs of Tokyo?

Yes. The 1945 survey covers central Tokyo, including Asakusa, where you can see the burnt ground left by the March 1945 air raids.

Is it free?

Yes. There is no account and no sign-up. It runs in a browser on a phone or a computer.

Where do the old photographs come from?

From the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan. The 1945-1950 series was flown by the US military, so it covers the cities they photographed. Where it is missing, the site falls back to the next oldest survey - 1961, 1974 and so on - and tells you which year you are looking at.

Why is half the screen unchanged in Kyoto?

Because Kyoto was not bombed. Comparing it with Tokyo or Hiroshima is the point.

Some articles are in Japanese.

Japanese Wikipedia has about four times as many articles on the ground in Japan. Where no English article exists, the button opens the Japanese one, and your browser can translate it.

Can I use it on my phone?

It is built for a phone first. You can also add it to your home screen and it will open full screen.

Does it cover the whole of Japan, or only the famous places?

The whole country. Beyond the 19 featured comparisons, zooming in shows 216,417 small local places - shrines, stations, monuments, old schools - across all 47 prefectures, drawn from OpenStreetMap, plus 2,469 disaster memorial stones recorded by the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan. The old aerial photographs work anywhere the surveys reached, not only on the featured spots.
